# Depression — Major Depressive Episode (DSM-5-TR)

## What depression is (DSM-5-TR framing)
In DSM-5-TR, a **major depressive episode** is a sustained period in which the person experiences clinically meaningful **depressed mood** and/or **markedly diminished interest or pleasure** (anhedonia), along with other depressive domains that represent a **change from previous functioning** and cause **distress or impairment** in social, occupational, or other important areas. The episode is defined by a cluster of domains present during the same period — commonly described as lasting about **two weeks or more** — rather than by a single bad day or brief low mood.

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is the diagnosis used when such an episode occurs and other conditions that better explain the picture (for example a bipolar course with mania or hypomania, or a substance/medical cause) are not the better account. This card supplies **vocabulary for recognising and discussing domains**; it is not a substitute for a clinician’s diagnostic decision or a file diagnosis.

## Gate features
Two features sit at the centre of the DSM-5-TR episode picture: **depressed mood** most of the day, nearly every day, and/or **loss of interest or pleasure** in activities. At least one of these gate features belongs in a classic major depressive episode description. Duration and functional impact complete the gate idea: the pattern is sustained, and it matters for day-to-day life (work or school, relationships, self-care).

## Depressive domains (Criterion A vocabulary)
Beyond the gate features, DSM-5-TR organises related domains that often travel with a major depressive episode:

- **Appetite or weight** — decrease or increase in appetite, or reported weight change  
- **Sleep** — insomnia or hypersomnia nearly every day  
- **Psychomotor change** — agitation or retardation, ideally noticeable to others as well as reported by the person  
- **Energy** — fatigue or loss of energy  
- **Worthlessness or guilt** — excessive or inappropriate guilt, or a sense of worthlessness (beyond ordinary self-reproach)  
- **Concentration** — diminished ability to think or concentrate, or indecisiveness  
- **Thoughts of death or suicide** — recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal ideation, plans, or attempts  

A full Criterion A picture in DSM-5-TR language usually involves several of these domains together with a gate feature. Community notes often emphasise **functional anchors** — attendance, self-care, sleep, social withdrawal — because those make the impact concrete.

## Related presentations and differentials
Not every low mood is a unipolar major depressive episode. **Manic or hypomanic** features (elevated or irritable mood, decreased need for sleep, racing thoughts, risky behaviour) point toward bipolar-spectrum language rather than unipolar MDD alone. **Substance use or medical conditions** may cause or mimic depressive domains and should be named when they are part of the reported picture. **Psychotic features** can accompany severe depression when present. **Bereavement and loss** can co-exist with a major depressive episode in DSM-5-TR; grief and depressive domains are related but not identical stories. **Persistent Depressive Disorder (dysthymia)** describes a longer-standing, more chronic low-mood pattern than a single discrete episode.

## Specifiers (vocabulary)
When a file already uses them, DSM-5-TR-style specifiers add colour to the episode: severity (mild, moderate, severe), anxious distress, mixed features, melancholic or atypical features, psychotic features, peripartum onset, seasonal pattern, and remission status. They are labels for nuance already established in the clinical record, not free-standing inventions.

## Care approaches often linked to depressive presentations
In community mental health, work with depressive presentations often includes psychoeducation, **behavioural activation**, CBT thought and behaviour methods, problem-solving, sleep-focused strategies (including CBT-I when sleep is the target), medication liaison with the clinic, and safety planning when risk is present. Transdiagnostic work may also target shared maintaining processes such as withdrawal, rumination, inactivity, and self-criticism loops. Formulation language (including 5P) often places cognitions, avoidance, and inactivity under perpetuating factors, and supports, routines, and reasons for living under protective factors — when those elements are part of the clinical understanding.

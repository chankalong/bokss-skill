# Bipolar — Manic, Hypomanic, and Depressive Episodes (DSM-5-TR)

## What bipolar spectrum presentations are (DSM-5-TR framing)
In DSM-5-TR, **bipolar and related disorders** are defined by the presence of **manic** or **hypomanic** episodes (current or past), often with major depressive episodes as well. A **manic episode** is a distinct period of abnormally and persistently elevated, expansive, or irritable mood and abnormally and persistently increased activity or energy, lasting about **one week or more** (or any duration if hospitalisation is required), with marked impairment, or psychotic features. A **hypomanic episode** is a similar mood–energy change lasting about **four consecutive days or more**, observable to others, **without** the marked impairment/hospitalisation threshold of mania. This card supplies **vocabulary for recognising and discussing domains**; it is not a substitute for a clinician’s diagnostic decision or a file diagnosis.

Bipolar I is typically associated with at least one manic episode; Bipolar II with hypomanic plus major depressive episodes without a manic episode. Cyclothymic patterns describe chronic fluctuating hypomanic and depressive symptoms that do not meet full episode thresholds. Community notes should stay conservative: elevated-mood language needs draft or file support.

## Gate features
Two poles organise the picture:

- **Mania / hypomania gate** — elevated, expansive, or **irritable** mood **plus** increased energy or activity, representing a change from usual functioning, noticed by others (especially for hypomania)
- **Depressive gate** (when present) — depressed mood and/or anhedonia with other depressive domains, as on the depression card

Duration, functional impact, and (for mania) possible need for hospitalisation or presence of psychotic features complete the gate idea. **Decreased need for sleep** (feeling rested on little sleep) is not the same as ordinary insomnia and is a high-value bipolar clue when evidenced.

## Episode domains (Criterion A vocabulary)
**Manic / hypomanic domains** often include:

- **Grandiosity** — inflated self-esteem or grandiosity  
- **Sleep need** — decreased need for sleep  
- **Speech** — more talkative than usual, or pressure to keep talking  
- **Thoughts** — flight of ideas or subjective racing thoughts  
- **Distractibility** — as reported or observed  
- **Activity / agitation** — increase in goal-directed activity or psychomotor agitation  
- **Risk-taking** — activities with high potential for painful consequences (spending, sexual, driving, investments) as concrete examples only  

**Depressive domains** (when the depressive pole is present) follow the depression card: appetite/weight, sleep, psychomotor change, energy, worthlessness/guilt, concentration, thoughts of death or suicide.

Community notes often emphasise **early warning signs** the person or family already named, medication changes with source (Clt vs clinic), and functional anchors (work, money, relationships, sleep pattern).

## Related presentations and differentials
Not every high energy or irritability is bipolar. **Unipolar MDD** lacks manic/hypomanic history. **Substance-induced** or **medication-induced** mood episodes (stimulants, steroids, antidepressants in some contexts) should be named when part of the reported picture. **ADHD** can share distractibility and impulsivity but lacks the distinct episodic mood–energy shift. **Borderline-spectrum** emotion dysregulation may show rapid mood shifts tied to interpersonal triggers rather than sustained multi-day mood–energy episodes. **Medical** causes of mood/energy change should be considered when evidenced. Do not label “hypomanic/manic” unless the draft or file already supports that language.

## Specifiers (vocabulary)
When a file already uses them, DSM-5-TR-style specifiers include severity, psychotic features, mixed features, anxious distress, rapid cycling, seasonal pattern, peripartum onset, and remission status. They are labels for nuance already established in the clinical record, not free-standing inventions.

## Care approaches often linked to bipolar presentations
In community mental health, work often includes psychoeducation on episode signatures and relapse prevention, sleep and routine stabilisation, medication liaison with the clinic (adherence, side effects, reported changes), safety planning when risk is present, and collaborative early-warning-sign plans with family when agreed. During depressive poles, behavioural activation and CBT elements may appear; during elevated poles, limit-setting around sleep, spending, and risk behaviours is often the priority. Formulation language (including 5P) often places sleep disruption, substance use, and medication interruption under precipitating/perpetuating factors when evidenced — and supports, insight into early signs, and reasons for living under protective factors.
